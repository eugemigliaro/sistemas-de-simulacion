Abstract. Since the subject of traﬃc dynamics has captured the interest of physi-
cists, many astonishing eﬀects have been revealed and explained. Some of the ques-
tions now understood are the following: Why are vehicles sometimes stopped by
so-called “phantom traﬃc jams”, although they all like to drive fast? What are the
mechanisms behind stop-and-go traﬃc? Why are there several diﬀerent kinds of
congestion, and how are they related? Why do most traﬃc jams occur considerably
before the road capacity is reached? Can a temporary reduction of the traﬃc vol-
ume cause a lasting traﬃc jam? Why do pedestrians moving in opposite directions
normally organize in lanes, while nervous crowds are “freezing by heating”? Why
do panicking pedestrians produce dangerous deadlocks? All these questions have
been answered by applying and extending methods from statistical physics and
non-linear dynamics to self-driven many-particle systems.


1    Introduction
Systems far from equilibrium are characterized by not being closed, but ex-
changing energy, particles, and information with their environment. Their
dynamics is often very complex, and usually there are no general results like
the ones from thermodynamics and statistical physics for gases, ﬂuids, and
solids in their stationary equilibrium state. This is partly due to the fact that
non-equilibrium systems can be quite diﬀerent in nature, dependent on the
respective interactions with their environment.
    Nevertheless, there exists a class of non-equilibrium systems for which
there is some hope for a uniﬁed description, despite of the rich variety of
observed phenomena. I am talking about self-driven or active many-particle
systems, in which the particles are in some sense “motorized” [1,2,3,4]. Ex-
amples are animal herds, ﬂocks of birds, or traﬃc. Sometimes, one can also
apply this approach to socio-economic systems, where shares, goods, or indi-
viduals correspond to the particles. Surprisingly enough, the most important
transport properties of such “particles” can be often taken into account by
two simple generalizations of Newton’s equation of motion.

3     Vehicle Traﬃc on Freeways

As a consequence, arbitrarily small perturbations, inhomogeneities, or ﬂuc-

with areas of high density and low velocity has evolved.
   The linearly stable regimes, in which the above condition is not fulﬁlled
and traﬃc is insensitive to small disturbances, are themselves divided into
• an absolutely stable regime, in which even large perturbations fade away,
• a metastable regime adjacent to the linearly unstable regime [7].
The metastable regime is characterized by the decay of those perturbations,
which are smaller than a certain density-dependent “critical amplitude”,
while larger perturbations are growing (“nucleation eﬀect”) [7,8].
    Stable traﬃc ﬂow is only found as long as the function V (s) changes little
with the distance s, i.e., when then the vehicle density is low (“free” traﬃc) or
high (since the velocity is anyway small, then). However, for traﬃc densities
around 25 vehicles per kilometer and lane (corresponding to velocities below
about 85 km/h), the “optimal velocity” is rapidly falling with decreasing
distance. Therefore, small changes are eventually ampliﬁed, until a traﬃc
jam is formed, even if there is no accident or bottleneck. One could say
that such a “phantom traﬃc jam” is the consequence of a chain reaction
based on individual over-reactions: The ﬁnite adaptation time τ causes a
delayed reaction to changes of the traﬃc situation, so that a vehicle gets too
close to its leading vehicle, which requires an over-braking. If the following
vehicles already arrive before the correspondingly increased disturbance can
be compensated for, the successive vehicles will all brake a little bit more.
In the end, traﬃc will reach a standstill, although all vehicles would like to
drive fast.
    If the inﬂow is not signiﬁcantly reduced, the traﬃc jam will not dissolve,
because accelerating vehicles lose time when they leave the downstream front
of congested traﬃc. The correspondingly increased time gaps imply a con-
siderably reduced outﬂow from congested traﬃc, compared to the maximum
ﬂow Qmax of homogeneous traﬃc deﬁning the theoretical capacity of the road.
As a consequence, the outﬂow Qout from congested traﬃc is a second, dy-
namical capacity, which is something like a self-organized “natural” constant
of traﬃc ﬂow [9].

3.2   The Rich Variety of Congested Traﬃc States
Most cases of traﬃc congestion on an n-lane freeway are observed upstream
of on-ramps or other bottlenecks. They can be triggered by perturbations

signiﬁcantly below the theoretical capacity, as soon as the sum of the up-
stream ﬂow Qup and the inﬂow ∆Q = Qin /n per lane exceeds the dynamical
capacity Qout : If a disturbance leads to temporary congestion, the drivers
must accelerate again and suﬀer some time delay, which reduces the capacity
to Qout . Therefore, the following vehicles will queue up, and the temporary
perturbation grows to form a persistent kind of congestion. The initial per-
turbation can even be a temporary reduction of the traﬃc ﬂow and/or vehicle
density, which can be caused by temporal variations of the traﬃc volume or
even by vehicles leaving the freeway at some oﬀ-ramp: When the traﬃc den-
sity has suﬃciently increased to reach the metastable regime, the “negative”
perturbation will be ampliﬁed if it only exceeds the critical amplitude. While
it is small, it will move downstream with the vehicles, so one could hope it
would pass the bottleneck and leave the system. However, when the density
wave grows larger, it will reduce its speed and even change its propagation di-
rection. Once it is fully developed, it moves upstream with constant velocity,
since vehicles leave the jam at the downstream front, while new ones join it at
the upstream front. Hence, the perturbation returns to the bottleneck like a
boomerang and triggers a breakdown of traﬃc, when it passes the bottleneck
in upstream direction, as it thereby reduces the eﬀective capacity to Qout .
    If the total traﬃc volume Qtot = (Qup + ∆Q) is greater than the dynamic
capacity Qout , we will automatically end up with a growing vehicle queue
upstream of the on-ramp. The traﬃc ﬂow Qcong resulting in the congested
area gives, together with the inﬂow ∆Q, exactly the outﬂow Qout , i.e. Qcong =
(Qout − ∆Q). One can distinguish the following cases [10] (see Fig. 1): If
the density cong associated with the ﬂow Qcong = Qf (cong ) is stable, we
ﬁnd homogeneous congested traﬃc (HCT) corresponding to typical traﬃc
jams during holiday seasons. For a smaller on-ramp ﬂow ∆Q, the congested
ﬂow Qcong is linearly unstable, and we either ﬁnd oscillating congested traﬃc
(OCT) or triggered stop-and-go traﬃc (TSG), which often emerges from a
spatial sequence of homogeneous and oscillating congested traﬃc (so-called
“pinch eﬀect” [11]). In contrast to OCT, stop-and-go traﬃc is characterized
by a sequence of moving jams, between which traﬃc ﬂows freely. Each traﬃc
jam triggers the next one by inducing a small perturbation at the ramp,
which propagates downstream as long as it is small, but turns back when
it has grown large enough (“boomerang eﬀect”). This, however, requires the
downstream traﬃc ﬂow to be linearly unstable. If it is metastable instead
(when the traﬃc volume is further reduced), a traﬃc jam will usually not
trigger a growing perturbation. In that case, one ﬁnds either a single moving
localized cluster (MLC), or a pinned localized cluster (PLC) at the location
of the ramp. The latter requires the traﬃc ﬂow in the upstream section to be
stable, so that no traﬃc jam can survive there. Finally, for suﬃciently small
traﬃc volumes, we ﬁnd free traﬃc (FT), as expected.

4.1   Lane Formation and “Freezing by Heating”
For normal, relaxed situations with small ﬂuctuation amplitudes ηi , our mi-
crosimulations of counterﬂows in corridors reproduce the empirically observed
formation of lanes consisting of pedestrians with the same desired walk-
ing direction, see Fig. 2a [14,15,4]). If we do not assume periodic bound-
ary conditions, these lanes are dynamically varying (see the Java applet
at http://www.helbing.org/Pedestrians/Corridor.html). Their number
depends on the width of the street [14], on pedestrian density, and on the
noise level. Interestingly, one ﬁnds a noise-induced ordering [16]: Compared
to small noise amplitudes, medium ones result in a more pronounced segre-
gation (i.e., a smaller number of lanes), while large noise amplitudes lead to
a “freezing by heating” eﬀect (see Fig. 2b).
    The above, symmetric model can explain lane formation without assum-
ing a preference for any side [15,17]. The most relevant point is the higher
relative velocity of pedestrians walking in opposite directions. As a conse-
quence, they have more frequent interactions until they have segregated into
separate lanes. The resulting collective pattern of motion minimizes the fre-
quency and strength of avoidance maneuvers, if ﬂuctuations are weak. This
state is related with a maximum eﬃciency of motion. One can ﬁnd such
optimal self-organization for a large class of driven many-particle systems.

would expect for equilibrium systems, and diﬀerent from ﬂuctuation-driven

precondition for the unusual freezing-by-heating transition are the additional
driving term vi0 e0i /τi and the dissipative friction −vi /τi , while the sliding

4.2   Collective Phenomena at Bottlenecks
In simulations of bottlenecks like doors, oscillatory changes of the passing
direction are observed, if people do not panic [19,18,4] (see Fig. 3). Once a
pedestrian is able to pass the narrowing, pedestrians with the same walking
direction can easily follow. Hence, the number and “pressure” of waiting and
pushing pedestrians becomes less than on the other side of the narrowing
where, consequently, the chance to occupy the passage grows. This leads to
a deadlock situation which is followed by a change in the passing direction.
Now, we will focus on uni-directional ﬂows at bottlenecks. The simulated
outﬂow from a room turns out to be well-coordinated and regular, if the
desired velocities vi0 = v0 are normal. However, for desired velocities above
1.5 m/s, i.e. for people in a rush, we ﬁnd an irregular succession of arch-like
blockings of the exit and avalanche-like bunches of leaving pedestrians, when
the arches break (see Fig. 4a,b). This phenomenon agrees with empirical
observations and is comparable to intermittent clogging found in granular
ﬂows through funnels or hoppers [12,13].

4.3   “Faster-Is-Slower Eﬀect” Due to Impatience
Since clogging is connected with delays, trying to move faster (i.e., increas-
ing vi0 ) can cause a smaller average speed of leaving, if the friction parameter
κ is large enough. This “faster-is-slower eﬀect” is particularly tragic in the
presence of ﬁres, where ﬂeeing people reduce their own chances of survival.
    Since our friction term has, on average, no deceleration eﬀect in the crowd
if the walls are suﬃciently remote, the arching underlying the clogging eﬀect
requires a combination of several eﬀects:
1. slowing down due to a bottleneck such as a door and
2. strong inter-personal friction, which becomes dominant when pedestrians
   get too close to each other.

4.4   “Phantom Panics”
Sometimes, panics have occured without any comprehensible reasons such
as a ﬁre or another threatening event (e.g., in Moscow, 1982; Innsbruck,
1999). Due to the “faster-is-slower eﬀect”, panics can be triggered by small
pedestrian counterﬂows [19,4,18], which cause delays to the crowd intend-
ing to leave. Consequently, stopped pedestrians in the back, who do not see
the reason for the temporary slowdown, are getting impatient and pushy. In
accordance with observations [20], one may describe this by increasing the
desired velocity, for example, according to the formula

5   Summary and Outlook

One can say that physics presently contributes signiﬁcantly to a deeper under-
standing of problems from traﬃc dynamics, biology, and the socio-economic
sciences. It has been found that the general methods from statistical physics
and non-linear dynamics are most useful in these areas. In particular, many
new insights into the often surprising phenomena in living systems have been
gained thanks to the relatively young paradigm of active many-particle sys-
tems, which was presented in a general mathematical framework, here.
    It would be desireable to develop a statistical mechanics of self-driven
many-particle systems on this basis, as it already exists for momentum- and
energy-conserving systems. Are there also general principles such as entropy
maximization or relations like the partition function, from which the prop-
erties of the respectively resulting macroscopic states can be derived? Up to
now, this has been answered only for speciﬁc non-equilibrium systems. How-
ever, this ﬁeld is not only of interest for fundamental research. Thanks to
various scientiﬁc projects with industrial partners, the insights from phys-
ical traﬃc theory are meanwhile adopted in traﬃc optimization strategies.
Examples are adaptive speed limits, intelligent on-ramp controls, incident
detection methods, traﬃc prognoses, and driver assistance systems. Active
many-particle systems are, therefore, a good example for a ﬁeld, in which
applied and fundamental research are close to each other. Moreover, many
results have quite general implications, in particular for biological and socio-
economic systems. They are partly transferable to individuals who compete
for limited ressources like time, money, energy, etc. [16,18].
