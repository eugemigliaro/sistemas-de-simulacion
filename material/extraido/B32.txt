1. Introduction

    The mechanisms of pedestrian navigation and collision avoidance are crucial in the dynamics of pedestrian movement in confined
and populated spaces. It is essential to have improved and experimentally validated models of such behavior for simulation software
to be useful in the design of pedestrian facilities, crowd management, urban planning, and safety.
    There are several types of navigation and collision avoidance models. One of the first ones is the Social Force Model (SFM),
which uses a repulsion force between particles to keep them from making contact [1,2]. However, while it is a way of avoiding
collision, it could also present some side effects [3–5]. The Predictive Collision Avoidance Model [5] proposes an improvement by
computing a different direction of the social repulsion based on future positions, thus producing more natural avoidance trajectories.
In contrast, Wang et al. [6] propose a new mechanism that removes the social force term and instead adjusts the magnitude and
direction of the desired velocity to achieve navigation.
    There are also other types of navigation models based on heuristics [7,8] where future movement is determined based on a fixed
set of rules or a series of nested discrete decisions. In velocity-based models, pedestrian dynamics are defined by determining future
velocity based on environmental elements [9,10]. Additionally, there are data-driven models [11,12] that utilize machine learning,
neural networks, or analysis of experimental data to determine particle movement.
    In a previous work [13], we introduced a comprehensive framework for simulating pedestrian navigation. In this framework,
simulated particles execute avoidance maneuvers exclusively by modifying their desired velocity, which is contingent on the
environment, including its geometry and the presence of other pedestrians. This premise is grounded in the belief that avoidance
behaviors in real-world scenarios can be effectively generated through the self-propulsion mechanism, typically represented by
desired velocity. It is worth noting that this reference framework is independent of the type of low-level model used, whether
rule-based, force-based, or otherwise.
    Using this approach, we rely on the contractile particle model (CPM) [14] to equip it with an avoidance mechanism that dy-
namically reorients its desired velocity. The rule-based pedestrian model proposed here not only successfully replicates fundamental

2. Experimental data

    We utilized data from our own experiments for one and two avoiding pedestrians, and from a public database from
Forschungszentrum Jülich for more crowded scenarios.
    The dilute scenario consisted of a collection of experimental trajectories obtained at the Motion Capture (MOCAP) Laboratory
at the Instituto Tecnológico de Buenos Aires (ITBA) [12]. The data are collected by instructing each pair of volunteers to stand
at different positions on a circle with a radius of 3 m and walk to the opposite side while avoiding the other volunteer in a
natural manner. The resulting 40 pairs of trajectories are displayed in Fig. 1(a). We also considered another set of 10 experimental
trajectories used in [12] in which individual volunteers were instructed to walk from one point of the same circle to the opposite
side while avoiding a fixed obstacle of approximately 0.3 m width at the center.
    The crowded scenario consisted of experimental trajectories published by the Forschungszentrum Jülich [15,16]. Specifically,
we analyzed the trajectories associated with a corridor of length 8 m and two different widths, 3 m and 3.6 m. We considered data
corresponding to bidirectional flow with the same flow rate in each direction. Fig. 1(b) shows the set of trajectories obtained in the
case of the lower input flow in both directions, and 3.6 m width.

3. Models

    In this study, we discuss three distinct models: the Contractile Particle Model (CPM) proposed by Baglietto and Parisi [14] that
we consider as the base model, and two modifications taking into account anisotropy and avoidance.
    The original CPM proposes that individual particles have a variable personal space radius. Whenever another particle enters this
personal space, the velocity of the initial particle reduces to zero only during one simulation time step, and the personal space radius
becomes equal to the particle’s physical and impenetrable radius. This reaction occurs independently of the direction through which
the invading particle enters the personal space, making the model isotropic. While this microscopic mechanism produces good results
at a macroscopic scale in unidirectional systems, it is not appropriate for bidirectional systems in which two near and parallel-moving
particles are unable to approach each other without triggering a reaction. To address this issue, we present a second model called
Anisotropic Contractile Particle Model (A-CPM), in which particles only react to personal space invasions that occur at the front of
their physical radius, which presents a narrower cross section than the personal space given by the current radius. The third variant
is a modification of A-CPM that enables particles to anticipate collisions and avoid them by performing evasive maneuvers. We call
this model Avoidance Anisotropic CPM (AA-CPM). In subsequent sections, we provide a detailed description of these models.

3.1. CPM

   The CPM [14] is an off-lattice cellular automaton, with particles occupying continuous positions in 2D space and rules for
updating their velocities.
   Each particle 𝑖 is defined by its position 𝐫𝑖 , a personal space radius 𝑟𝑖 ∈ [𝑟𝑚𝑖𝑛 , 𝑟𝑚𝑎𝑥 ], and a desired velocity 𝐯𝑑 pointing towards
a given target 𝐓𝑖 . The particle’s radius should only be interpreted as a hard physical radius when it is at its minimum value (𝑟𝑚𝑖𝑛 ).
But for larger radii, it represents a personal space needed for walking. The magnitude of the particle’s velocity is a function of 𝑟𝑖 .
When the personal space radius is at its maximum, the particle moves at the desired speed 𝑣𝑑𝑚𝑎𝑥 .

3.2. A-CPM

    While the original CPM satisfactorily reproduces observed data for unidirectional flows, it presents difficulties when attempting
to simulate bidirectional flow because the cross-sectional area of the isotropic particle (for 𝑟 > 𝑟𝑚𝑖𝑛 ) is higher than in real pedestrian
systems, resulting in artificial collisions and decreased speed. To overcome this issue, we propose the anisotropic CPM (A-CPM),
which has different contact detection criteria. Inspired by the work of Tordeux et al. [17], we defined that particles in the A-CPM
collide only within a frontal margin, making it a more permissive and asymmetric approach.
    We consider that a particle enters in contact with another if the personal space in front of the particle overlaps with the personal
space of the other, as illustrated in Fig. 2(b). Formally, a particle 𝑖, with position 𝐫𝑖 and velocity 𝐯𝑖 , is in contact with particle 𝑗,
with position 𝐫𝑗 and personal space radius 𝑟𝑗 , if either of the following conditions holds, depending on 𝑟𝑖 :

    • 𝑟𝑖 = 𝑟𝑚𝑖𝑛 and |𝐫𝑖 − 𝐫𝑗| < 𝑟𝑖 + 𝑟𝑗 ; or
    • 𝑟𝑖 ≠ 𝑟𝑚𝑖𝑛 and the angle 𝛽 formed between 𝐯𝑖 and 𝐫𝑗 − 𝐫𝑖 lies between −𝜋∕2 and 𝜋∕2.
      Moreover, the following two conditions must be satisfied simultaneously:

           – The lines parallel to 𝐯𝑖 from the endpoints to the circle of radius 𝑟𝑚𝑖𝑛 intersect the circle centered at 𝐫𝑗 with radius 𝑟𝑗 .
           – The two particles overlap in their current radii, that is, |𝐫𝑖 − 𝐫𝑗 | < 𝑟𝑖 + 𝑟𝑗 .

    To illustrate these conditions, in Fig. 2(a) we can observe that particle ℎ fulfills only the first condition, as indicated by the
two marked black dots. Meanwhile, particle 𝑗 satisfies solely the second condition due to the overlap. As a result, neither of these
particles is considered to be colliding with particle 𝑖. On the other hand, in Fig. 2(b) we can see an example of a particle that, by
simultaneously meeting both conditions, we consider to be colliding with particle 𝑖.
    In the A-CPM, a particle collides with a wall only if the distance to the nearest point on the wall is less than its minimum radius
𝑟𝑚𝑖𝑛 .

4. Results

4.2. Unidirectional and bidirectional flow

    In this section, we explore the corridor scenario from [18], where we deliberately selected the particle–wall avoidance parameters
𝐴𝑤 and 𝐵𝑤 through a qualitative process. Specifically, we set 𝐴𝑤 = 1215 and 𝐵𝑤 = 0.025. With this choice, when a particle grazes
the wall, the magnitude of the collision vector is approximately |𝐧𝑤    𝑐 | ∼ 10, while at a distance of 0.3 m from the wall (twice the
minimum radius), the magnitude decreases to |𝐧𝑤     𝑐 | ≲ 0.1. These values  enable particles to prevent overlapping their hard physical
cores (𝑟𝑚𝑖𝑛 ) with the walls while also allowing them to make full use of the corridor width, thus ensuring that artificial distancing
from the walls is not introduced.

rectional flows in a corridor. The results are then compared with experimental data and with the other two models, serving as
benchmarks.

density, and the corresponding pedestrian is counted as one of the 𝑘 neighbors. The area 𝐴𝑘 is the intersection between the interior
of the corridor and the circle centered at the particle with a radius equal to the distance from the particle to the 𝑘th nearest neighbor.
We selected 𝑘 = 5 based on the findings in Ref. [21], which reported a difference of less than 0.35% compared to the Voronoi cell
method [22]. This procedure allows us to obtain points (𝜌𝑖 (𝑡𝑖 ), 𝑣𝑖 (𝑡𝑖 )) for all particles and time steps. From these microscopic data,
we compute the fundamental diagram by moving average with 𝑛 = 10000.
     Fig. 4(e) presents curves corresponding to both simulated systems and experimental data, as reported in the bibliography [23,24].
It is evident that the AA-CPM exhibits a trend more similar to the experimental speed-density relationships. In contrast, the A-CPM,
which employs a relaxed collision criterion, results in fewer reductions in particle speed compared to both CPM and AA-CPM. The
lower speed observed in AA-CPM may be attributed to avoidance maneuvers, leading to subsequent lateral movements that do not
allow achieving the maximum possible displacement.

there is no actual overlap. The overlaps that are observed between particles are indicators that in the next time step, the particles

4.3. Room evacuation

   After verifying the satisfactory performance of the AA-CPM in emulating experimental data from various scenarios, we aimed to
investigate whether the model preserves the original CPM properties concerning the specific flow rate through doors.
   To this end, we conducted simulations using the AA-CPM, in which 𝑁 particles evacuate from a room of dimensions 20 m × 20 m
through an exit door located horizontally at the center of the point (10, 0) with a variable width 𝐿. The target at the door for a
particle with coordinates (𝑥, 𝑦) is determined as follows:

    • The point (𝑥, −3) if 𝑦 ≤ 0.5 and 10 − 0.4𝐿 ≤ 𝑥 ≤ 10 + 0.4𝐿.
    • The point (𝑥𝑟 , 0) is chosen with 𝑥𝑟 uniformly distributed between 10 − 0.4𝐿 and 10 + 0.4𝐿, otherwise.

   The initial positions of the particles are randomly selected within the room with a minimum radius, and other simulation
parameters are identical to those used in Section 4.1. The number of particles used is 𝑁 = {200, 300, 400, 500, 600}, for values
of 𝐿 = (0.2 + 0.005 𝑁) m depending on the number of particles, consistent with the method specified in [27] for calculating egress
capacity. Ten realizations are performed for each 𝑁. In Fig. 8(a) we present the system state with 500 particles at 4 s after the
simulation started. In this case, it is also possible to observe slight overlaps with the walls. This is due to the more relaxed collision
detection criterion for walls in the AA-CPM.
   The average specific flow rate for each case is calculated as 𝑄𝑒 = 𝑁∕(𝐿 𝑇𝑒 ), where 𝑇𝑒 represents the total evacuation time. Fig. 8(b)
depicts the obtained results, indicating that the specific flow rate remains almost independent of the number of people, similar to
the original CPM [14], with values falling within the empirical range reported in the literature [28–32] between 𝑄𝑒 = 1.25 (m s)−1
and 𝑄𝑒 = 2 (m s)−1 .

5. Conclusions

    The CPM is a recognized off-lattice cellular automaton that incorporates rules governing the movement of simulated pedestrians
in continuous space. It has been shown to satisfactorily reproduce experimental unidirectional fundamental diagrams and specific
flow rates [14]. In this research, we extend its capabilities through two modifications: (a) introducing Anisotropy in particle
collisions, and (b) implementing Avoidance mechanisms that dynamically adjust desired velocities to prevent collisions. We refer
to this new model as AA-CPM.
    The parameters shared with the CPM were directly inherited. The new parameters related to the avoidance mechanism were
calibrated using experimental data from pedestrian-pedestrian and pedestrian-obstacle trajectories. After fixing the parameters of
the pedestrian model, it was successfully tested against experimental data from various scenarios encompassing different levels of
complexity: (a) individual trajectories; (b) unidirectional flow; (c) bidirectional flow; and (d) egress through narrow doors. The study
includes validation through the following observables: (1) mean distance between simulated and real trajectories; (2) fundamental
diagrams; (3) cluster structure; and (4) specific flow rates through exits.

    While the CPM can accurately simulate scenarios (b) and (d), the new AA-CPM not only maintains this ability but also excels

these scenarios.
    An important aspect of the new model is that it is not necessary to turn the avoidance mechanism on or off. When it begins to
fail at high densities, the low-level A-CPM comes into play, and thus the behavior of the dense assembly matches experimental data.
    As is customary in pedestrian dynamics modeling, this model accurately reproduces the observed phenomena within the
examined parameter space. However, extending the application of the model to encompass additional observables or varying
parameter ranges should be undertaken judiciously, requiring a rigorous validation process involving new experimental comparisons.
